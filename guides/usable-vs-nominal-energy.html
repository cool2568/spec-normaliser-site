Usable energy vs nominal energy in battery datasheets

Nominal energy is what the cells hold on paper; usable energy is what the battery management system lets you take out. On the datasheets behind our comparison pages the gap runs from 5% to 11%, so normalise to usable energy before you compare sizes or prices.

Two numbers, two meanings

Nominal energy, also printed as rated energy, energy capacity or nominal capacity, is the cells' rated charge times the battery's nominal voltage. The Dyness Powerbox F-2.5 is 50 Ah at 48 V, which is 2.4 kWh; the Pylontech US5000's 4.8 kWh at 48 V works out to 100 Ah. A figure in ampere-hours is a charge, not an energy, so it compares only with a battery of the same voltage: multiply by the nominal voltage to get watt-hours.

Usable energy, also printed as usable capacity, available energy or energy (useable), is what can be discharged between the limits the battery management system (BMS) enforces, at the rate and temperature the manufacturer tested. GoodWe measures the 4.8 kWh of its Lynx Home U LX U5.4-L at 90% depth of discharge, 0.5C and 25 °C; BYD measures the 5.12 kWh of its Battery-Box Premium HVS 5.1 as DC usable energy at 100% depth of discharge, 0.2C and 25 °C.

Depth of discharge (DoD) is the share of nominal capacity used in one cycle. If a sheet gives nominal energy and DoD but no usable figure, usable energy is approximately nominal energy × DoD.

Why manufacturers quote both

Keeping a cell away from its fully charged and fully discharged voltage limits slows its ageing, so many systems hold back a margin at one or both ends. The nominal figure tells you how much cell capacity you are paying for; the usable figure tells you how much energy you will get. Sheets differ in which they lead with. BYD and Sungrow give only a usable figure, and Sungrow describes its depth of discharge as "Max. 100% DOD (settable)". Tesla gives a nominal battery energy of 13.5 kWh for the Powerwall 3, stated as AC, after its built-in inverter.

A depth of discharge can also be a recommendation rather than a limit. The Dyness Powerbox F-2.5's 2.28 kWh of usable energy is 95% of its 2.4 kWh nominal, but the same sheet recommends 90% depth of discharge, which would give 2.16 kWh, and its 6000-cycle figure is tested at 90%.

Normalising two datasheets

  1. Take the usable energy if the sheet gives it; otherwise use nominal energy × DoD, and note that you did.
  2. Convert ampere-hours to kilowatt-hours with the nominal voltage.
  3. Check the boundary: energy at the battery's DC terminals is not the same as AC energy after an inverter, which already includes conversion losses.
  4. Check the conditions: the rate, the temperature, beginning of life, and whether the DoD is a limit or a recommendation.
  5. Price on usable kilowatt-hours, and for lifetime cost multiply usable energy by the warranted cycles or energy throughput.

FAQ

What is depth of discharge?

The share of a battery's nominal capacity used in one cycle. A 10 kWh battery cycled at 90% depth of discharge delivers about 9 kWh per cycle.

Can usable energy be higher than nominal energy?

Not when both are measured the same way. If a sheet shows usable energy above nominal, the two figures come from different conditions or definitions, so ask the manufacturer which applies.

Which figure should I use to compare prices?

Usable energy, measured at the same point (DC or AC) on both sheets. Price per usable kWh is the fair basis; price per nominal kWh flatters a battery that holds back more of its capacity.
